# Warm-up design

Every warm-up, whatever its style (`lesson-style` §1 and §3):
- **Review only:** a skill or idea today's problems need. It doesn't preview the new idea in a way that needs teaching.
- **Low cognitive load:** nearly every student can answer it in about 3 minutes. It is the most scaffolded part of the lesson.
- **Short:** one item, or 2-3 very quick ones.
- **Answered in the key** (a sample answer when answers vary).

Record the chosen style in the blueprint row (`warm-up style: ...`) so the writer and the audit know which one it is.

## Styles

### A. Scaffolded review (the usual choice)

Values given, a fill-in or choices, and a figure to look at. Use it when the lesson needs a specific procedure warmed up, such as the Pythagorean theorem, solving a proportion, counting grid units from a center, or labeling sides from an angle.
- Example (4.10): "Find each angle with the sin⁻¹, cos⁻¹, or tan⁻¹ key. Round to the nearest degree." Three labeled boxes, the keys named.
- Example (3.13): one right triangle with two sides labeled, "x =" box, and the formula printed in the prompt.

### B. Intuition check with ready-made examples (optional)

Give students examples or already-made constructions they should know about, and ask questions they should know the answers to. Ideally they can just give intuitive answers, without focusing on the math terminology. Don't use this for every warm-up; it fits some lessons and not others.

**When it fits:**
- Today's core idea has an everyday intuition students already hold: same shape vs. same size, steeper vs. flatter, matching vs. not matching, folding onto itself, stretched vs. scaled, safe vs. unsafe.
- The review is a judgment, not a computation.
- You want every student, including newcomers to English, to have something to say in the first minutes.

**How to write it:**
- Show the thing, already made: two drawn triangles, a dilation already drawn with its image, a figure folded along a line, two photos (one scaled, one stretched), a ramp drawn at two slopes, a shape and its rotated copy. Students look; they don't construct or compute.
- Ask plain questions they can answer from intuition: "Which copy looks stretched?", "Are these the same size?", "Which ramp is easier to push a cart up?", "Would this shape land on itself if you flipped it?", "Which two match?"
- The answer is a choice, a yes/no, circling or pointing, or a few words. Use labeled boxes with the choices printed ("(A or B)", "(yes or no)").
- Don't require the new vocabulary, or any vocabulary, to answer. The math words come later, in Together and the QuickNotes, attached to the intuition students just used.
- Optionally add one "How can you tell?" box that accepts everyday words ("it looks squished", "they're the same but bigger").
- The key gives the answer and a sample everyday explanation. The plan says which student words to connect to the lesson's term later ("you said 'squished': today we call that not a scaled copy").

**Examples from our lessons:**
- 3.1: an original photo and two edited copies, "Which copy is the scaled copy? Which is stretched?" with boxes "(A or B)".
- 2.7: two triangles with the same two angles drawn at different sizes, "Are the triangles congruent? (yes or no)".
- 4.1: two ramps drawn at different angles, "Which is steeper? Which would you rather push a wheelchair up?"

**Why it works for Period 4:** everyday language and gestures count as mathematical reasoning (Moschkovich, `mll-literacy-scaffolding`). Students start the lesson by being right about something, and the vocabulary has an intuition to attach to.

### C. Notice and wonder / which one doesn't belong

A low-floor look at one or four images. It works best when the image sets up today's question. Use `columns:` with "I notice..." / "I wonder..." headers, or four labeled panels with one box. Keep it to 3 minutes, and put sample answers in the key.

## Choosing

| If today's lesson... | Try |
|---|---|
| needs a computation or procedure warmed up | A |
| builds on an intuition students already have | B |
| opens with a question best raised by an image | C |

When unsure, use A. Don't switch every lesson to B; vary it when the lesson's core idea has a natural intuition.
